2.1 Introduction to the Requirements

Elicitation: Requirements came from three sources: the Product Brief and Assessment Paper (ENG1 Group Assessment 1 and 2), team meetings, and customer feedback. Meeting 2 (5 October) reviewed the assessment and prepared customer questions. Meeting 3 (7 October) recorded gameplay ideas and questions. Meeting 4 (12 October) allocated the requirements work. Meeting 5 (14 October) required requirement IDs to link to the architecture, and Meeting 7 added the annoyance meter, score tracker, and peck behaviour. After 22 October the team worked mainly through Discord and shared documents, with later changes recorded in the other deliverables.

Negotiation: Questions were prepared on escape, pausing, items, events, usability, and deployment. On 8 October, the customer replied that geese should not be harmed by the player, that the game is to start paused, and that about 90% of the bread may be used [C]. SYS-07, SYS-15, and SYS-16 were updated accordingly. Where no reply was available for the goose cap and peck rule, labelled assumptions were recorded (End of 2b) and each requirement was given a testable value that can change if the customer disagrees. Assessment 1 requires two initial items, two hidden events, a timer, and counters, so everything beyond that is marked A2 [A, P].

Presentation & Referencing System: User requirements (USR) state what the player needs, and system requirements (SYS) state the observable behaviour, each linked to its parent user requirement and accompanied by an objective test (following NASA guidance that requirements be unique, verifiable, and traceable [N]). Non-functional requirements (NFR) cover quality and delivery constraints. IDs carry the prefix REQ- (omitted in tables below) and are never reused; each ID is cited in Architecture (Arch1, Section 3.5), code comments, and test names (T-SYS-01, etc.).

Basis Key: Brief = Product Brief [P] or Assessment [A] | Customer = Customer Reply [C] | Team = Decision in Meeting Minutes [M] or Team Design.

3.1 Overview and Notation

The architecture is described using UML 2.0 with three diagrams: a class diagram for structure (Figure 1), a sequence diagram of the player collecting the bus ticket (Figure 2), and a state machine of the game from start to win or lose (Figure 3).

  • Lucidchart for the class diagram: easy to understand and supports real-time multi-member collaboration.
  • PlantUML for the sequence diagram and state machine: text-based notation suited for version-controlled dynamic UML diagrams.

3.2 Architectural Approach

Main Principle: Game logic is decoupled from rendering and input. Game rules (annoyance level, inventory, hidden events, and win/lose conditions) reside in pure logic classes containing no graphics code (REQ-NFR-06 / REQ-NFR-07), while libGDX handles Tiled map loading, assets, drawing, input, audio, and the camera.

  1. Testing: Game rules can be unit-tested without launching a graphics window.
  2. Teamwork: Team members can develop game logic and graphics concurrently.
  3. Extension: New items or hidden events can be added in Assessment 2 without rewriting core rule classes.
  4. Clarity: Single-responsibility classes keep the codebase readable and traceable.

3.3 Structural Architecture (17 Classes)

GameEngine is the central class owning the main components and running the game loop, including pauseGame() and resumeGame() (REQ-USR-02, REQ-SYS-02). The remaining classes fall into five groups:

  • Rules: GameState (win, lose, and counters), Timer, and AnnoyanceBar.
  • World and Entities: CampusMap, PlayerCharacter and Goose (both extend Entity), and GooseHerd.
  • Items: Inventory, Item, BreadCrumbs, and BusTicket.
  • Events: HiddenEvent and LongBoiStatue.
  • Display: UserInterface and WaypointMarker.

UML Relationships: Composition (GameEngine owns GameState, Timer, AnnoyanceBar, CampusMap, PlayerCharacter, GooseHerd, UserInterface, and 0..* HiddenEvent; PlayerCharacter owns Inventory), Aggregation (GooseHerd groups 1..* Goose; Inventory holds 0..* Item), Inheritance (PlayerCharacter/Goose → Entity; BreadCrumbs/BusTicket → Item; LongBoiStatue → HiddenEvent), and Dependency (e.g., BreadCrumbs disperses GooseHerd; Goose raises AnnoyanceBar).

3.4 Behavioural Architecture

Collecting the Bus Ticket (Sequence Diagram — Figure 2):

  1. The player moves PlayerCharacter (REQ-USR-01). On each update, GameEngine asks LongBoiStatue (a HiddenEvent) to run checkTrigger(PlayerCharacter) (REQ-SYS-13) and checks via GooseHerd whether all geese are dispersed (REQ-SYS-07).
  2. If the player is at the trigger point, the event has not been used, and all geese are dispersed, execute() runs giveItem(), creating and returning the BusTicket (REQ-SYS-16).
  3. GameEngine passes the ticket to PlayerCharacter, which stores it via Inventory.addItem(BusTicket).

State Machine of the Game (Figure 3):

  • Starts in Paused (freezes Timer and geese); transitions between Paused and Running via resume / pause (REQ-USR-02, REQ-SYS-02).
  • Transitions to Won when GameState.checkWinCondition() is true (all geese dispersed and player holds BusTicket, REQ-SYS-07).
  • Transitions to Lost when Timer expires or AnnoyanceBar is full (REQ-SYS-14).

5.1 Risk Management Process

Our team follows a simple four-step process suitable for a small, non-critical software project with a fixed deadline:

  1. Identify: Risks are identified through team discussions and categorised as Project, Technology, or Product risks.
  2. Assess: Each risk is rated for Likelihood (L) and Impact (I).
  3. Plan: The team agrees mitigation measures that limit impact, avoid single-person dependencies, and assign clear owner(s).
  4. Monitor: Owners reassess their risks, and the full register is reviewed at each weekly meeting to close resolved risks, update ratings, and add new ones.

Risk Rating Scale: Both Likelihood and Impact use a 3-level scale: Low (1), Medium (2), or High (3). Rating = Likelihood × Impact (1–2 = Minor, 3–4 = Moderate, 6–9 = Serious).

Level Likelihood Impact
Low (1) Unlikely to happen during the project Small delay or rework, no effect on marks
Medium (2) May happen once Noticeable delay or loss of quality in one deliverable
High (3) Expected to happen or already likely Missed deadline, or a failed or incomplete deliverable
